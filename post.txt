Before the engine can play anything the evaluator has to be trained, so I started by pulling 500,000 positions out of a database of about 940,000 real over-the-board games, skipping the opening moves of each game and dropping duplicates so a position reached by a different move order only counts once. That gives me the inputs but not the answers, so every one of those positions goes to Stockfish with a fixed budget of 50,000 nodes and whatever evaluation it hands back becomes the label. Labelling is the expensive half and it is pure CPU work, because Stockfish is an alpha-beta search and a GPU cannot help it at all, so I split it across 224 cores and the whole 500,000 finished in about 21 minutes instead of the day and a half it would have taken on my laptop. The network itself is deliberately small, 781 inputs into 128 into 32 into one output, about 104,000 parameters, and it never learns chess from scratch because it only learns a correction on top of plain material counting, with the targets squashed through tanh so a single mate score cannot swamp the gradient. The train/validation split is by game rather than by position, since positions from the same game are nearly identical and would otherwise leak across and make the validation number lie to me. The first version of that model was a genuinely better evaluator than the one before it and still lost badly, which took me a while to work out: its evaluation was more accurate but noisier between plies, so alpha-beta got fewer cutoffs and it needed 43% more nodes to reach the same depth, and losing most of a ply costs far more than the accuracy was worth. Scaling the learned correction down to half fixed it, and that version wins 9 out of 10 against Stockfish set to its weakest 1320 rating on an equal clock.

The playing side is the part people usually assume is the network, and it mostly is not. The network never outputs a move, it only scores a position, so choosing what to play is a normal alpha-beta search with iterative deepening, a transposition table, null-move pruning, late move reductions, check extensions, killer and history move ordering, and a quiescence search that keeps looking at captures past the horizon so it does not stop counting in the middle of a trade. The one trick worth mentioning is that the first layer is never recomputed from scratch: the features are relative to whoever is moving, so I keep two running accumulators, one per point of view, and a move only adds and subtracts the handful of features it actually changed. That is worth more than it sounds, because the extra depth it buys was measured at about +230 Elo in head-to-head games, and the search rewrite before it was worth another +190. I put the whole thing on a page so you can play it and watch what it is doing at the same time, at https://fruit-fly-chess.vercel.app/ — it shows the evaluation split into material and the network's correction, both hidden layers firing per position, the input planes as the network actually sees them, the search depth and node count and the line it expects, a 3D view of the activations, and a list of every legal move scored by the network alone with no look-ahead. That last list is the interesting one, because the move at the top of it is very often not the move it plays. The short version of why all these steps exist: the network is only a judge of positions and a fairly rough one, so the search, the move ordering, the accumulator and the calibration all exist to turn those rough judgements into moves that are actually good.
